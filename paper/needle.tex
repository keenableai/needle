\begin{abstract}
AI agents call web search APIs millions of times a day, yet no public
benchmark tracks how well those APIs retrieve, and any fixed query set goes
stale or gets tuned against. We present \needle, an open-source benchmark that draws its queries and
gold from public sources on every run (rare: a fixed stream), and a
measurement study of the 26 search API configurations it has run: hourly news queries from RSS feeds and Google Trends, and daily
known-item or fact queries from six public registries and archives. An
LLM judge or a deterministic matcher scores each result, so no
annotators are needed. A pooled ``ceiling'' normalizes every bench: engines are compared as a
share of an oracle list pooled from all of them. On the 13 days when all
21 current engines ran, shares of the ceiling span 14--73\%, weekly
leaderboards are stable, body-detail queries separate engines far more
than title queries, and a \texttt{site:} restriction on the same caselaw
captions costs a third of recall. Blinded raters of three vendors agree
with each other more than any agrees with the judge ($\kappa$ .90--.94
against .77--.79); every scorer is audited by LLMs, not humans. Paired
tests separate most engine pairs, but the leaders form one
neighbourhood, and on the one day scored after the rules were frozen
Perplexity edges past Keenable, so readers should choose by group and by
bench, not by rank. Code, data and dashboard: \url{https://keenableai.github.io/needle}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Agents rely on retrieval APIs that return a handful of URLs with titles
and snippets; the choice shapes what the agent can know, yet developers
pick on anecdote and price, because evidence is thin. Public IR test collections target document rankers, not live web
indexes, and their labels age (``Fed rate decision'' changes every six
weeks).
Agent benchmarks such as BrowseComp score the whole agent, so a weak retriever hides
behind a strong model.

\needle takes a different route (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Its unit
of evaluation is one API call: a query in, up to $k$ results out, as the
agent sees them. Every run \emph{generates} its queries from public sources, with a gold
identity where one exists and a judged relevance profile otherwise, so
no fixed test set accumulates beyond the rare stream and recurring
registry facts (Limitations). Where gold has
a canonical identity (an arXiv id, a reporter citation, a 10-Q figure), a
deterministic matcher checks the returned URLs and snippets; where
relevance is graded (news), an LLM judge rates each result and an ideal
list pooled across engines normalizes the score. Scores rest on snippets
and, for two benches, on one judge; we audit both (Limitations).

Contributions, each a claim the reader can check:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=1.2em]
\item A query recipe for five domains that yields fresh gold or judged
  relevance without annotators: three benches match gold identities deterministically
  (finance with an LLM backstop), and on the two judged
  benches two blinded sessions of one LLM (Claude Opus 5.5) that read a
  page excerpt agree with the judge at $\kappa$ .77 (\S\ref{sec:scoring}, Appendix~\ref{app:checks}).
\item A scoring design that mixes a graded judge with exact matching and
  normalizes by a pooled ceiling; removing any one engine from the pool
  moves the overall share by at most .007 (\S\ref{sec:scoring}).
\item Four months of public results for 26 configurations: shares span
  14--73\% of the ceiling, and body-detail queries spread engines from .08 to .72
  (\S\ref{sec:results}, Figure~\ref{fig:results}, Table~\ref{tab:results}).
\end{itemize}
The combination is new; its scoring ingredients follow prior work
(\S\ref{sec:related}).

\section{Related Work and Design Rationale}
\label{sec:related}

Each strand of prior work below fixes one design decision in \needle.

\paragraph{Fresh queries beat fixed test sets.} Static benchmarks leak into
training data and get tuned against \citep{sainz2023trouble,balloccu2024leak}.
LiveBench, LiveCodeBench, RealTime QA, FreshQA and LiveSearchBench draw
new items from dated sources or Wikidata deltas
\citep{white2025livebench,jain2025livecodebench,kasai2023realtimeqa,vu2024freshllms,zhou2025livesearchbench,thakur2025freshstack};
all score an answering model, or in FreshStack's case a retriever, over
a fixed corpus. \needle borrows the idea but studies the live
retrievers.

\paragraph{Agents need the retriever measured alone.} BrowseComp, GAIA and AssistantBench measure an agent end to end
\citep{wei2025browsecomp,mialon2024gaia,yoran2024assistantbench};
BrowseComp-Plus freezes a corpus to separate retriever from agent
\citep{chen2026browsecompplus}, and the closest API study swaps three
providers under one agent on a static set \citep{selvam2026search}. Agent query logs show quoted phrases and operators are common
\citep{meng2026textranking,pezzuti2026agentssearch}, so half of
\needle's finance and legal queries carry operators, each engine receives them through its native filter where one exists
(Appendix~\ref{app:ops}), the news judge enforces them literally, and the
known-item matchers accept the gold identity only on the requested host. Web search audits and overlap studies take one engine set at a few
points in time, with fixed queries
\citep{lewandowski2015evaluating,bevendorff2024google,kirsten2026characterizing,yagci2022overlap};
\needle repeats a multi-API evaluation with regenerated tasks every run.

\paragraph{LLM judges work at the system level, with known failure modes.}
LLM assessors reproduce system rankings at TREC scale
\citep{thomas2024llms,upadhyay2024umbrela,upadhyay2024largescale,arabzadeh2025benchmarking},
so \needle reports engine-level means only. Known risks shape the rest: circularity with LLM-based systems
\citep{clarke2024llmbased,balog2025rankers}, keyword stuffing
\citep{alaofi2024fooled}, a ceiling at the judge's competence
\citep{soboroff2025dont,faggioli2023perspectives}, and snippet labels that
diverge from full-text labels \citep{kazai2022less}; so identical results are judged once and snippets are capped uniformly.

\paragraph{Known-item gold is free and synthetic queries are valid.}
Simulated known-item queries date to \citet{azzopardi2007simulated}, and
LLM-elicited tip-of-tongue queries \citep{arguello2021tot} rank systems
like real ones \citep{he2025totelicitation}. Doc2query, InPars and Promptagator generate queries from documents
\citep{nogueira2019doc2query,bonifacio2022inpars,dai2023promptagator};
\needle does the same, with leak filters. Citation graphs give annotation-free gold for caselaw
\citep{hou2025clerc}, and full text beats abstracts \citep{lin2009fulltext}: the scholar buckets measure that effect
live.

\section{The Benchmark}

\subsection{Query generation}

\textbf{News.} Each hour an LLM writes a terse query on the specific
event of the newest item from each of 124 feeds and of the Google Trends
RSS for 52 US geographies, and refuses evergreen items; each run then
samples 20 queries round-robin over topical domains. \textbf{Finance.} Registry facts (CEO, founding year, headquarters,
employees, LEI, ticker) come from Wikidata and GLEIF; quarterly revenue,
net income, operating income and diluted EPS from SEC XBRL filings; a
third set asks for a filing by description.
\textbf{Scholar.} Each paper yields a \emph{title} query with case and punctuation stripped;
a \emph{body keyword} query on a detail absent from title and abstract;
a \emph{body question} asking the same fact in prose; and a
\emph{tip-of-tongue} description with every name and exact value banned. A paper enters only if all four pass, so the buckets are paired.
\textbf{Legal.} Recent opinions from 14 federal courts yield one caption-style
query each; gold is the citation, docket number and CourtListener id. Sections of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) yield an LLM query
built on one verbatim span of two to eight words, quoted in one variant in
four; a query
that leaks the citation is rejected (Appendix~\ref{app:leak}). \textbf{Rare.}
English agent queries from a public stream, with a word WordPiece splits
into five or more pieces.

\subsection{Scoring}
\label{sec:scoring}

\textbf{Graded relevance with a pooled ceiling.} For news and rare queries an
LLM judge (GPT-6 at temperature 0) sees today's date, the query, a
precomputed per-query profile (Appendix~\ref{app:judge}) and one result
(title, URL, date, capped snippet), and outputs a 0--4 rating adapted
from the Needs Met scale of Google's rater guidelines \citep{google2025sqrg},
with gates for integrity, entity, coverage, freshness and staleness,
caps for sensitive domains, and literal compliance with quoted phrases
and \texttt{site:}. Gains are $\{4\!:\!1,\,3\!:\!0.667,\,2\!:\!0.117,\,1\!:\!0,\,0\!:\!0\}$
(top-heavy, after ERR \citep{chapelle2009err}) and repeated URLs score
zero \citep{clarke2008novelty}. The \ult is the best rating per deduplicated URL pooled across all
engines, oracle-ordered.
A judged query no engine answers with a rating of 2 or more is dropped;
known-item benches keep every query (Appendix~\ref{app:example}).

\textbf{Identifier and answer matching.} Scholar and legal scan result URLs
and snippets for arXiv ids, DOIs, PMIDs, reporter citations, docket numbers and CFR citations; recall@$k$ is the share of queries whose top-$k$ results carry the gold
identity; for caselaw the identity is the case (opinion, docket page or a citing
page), so the metric is case-identity recall
(Appendix~\ref{app:checks}). Finance asks whether the top-$k$ snippets
\emph{contain the answer}: nickname-tolerant names, a 0.1\% band for money (table figures and
parenthesized losses included) and 15\% for headcounts, country aliases,
domain match, and cue words for low-cardinality fields (year, country) so
a stray number cannot score. An LLM backstop may upgrade rejected non-money results, never revoke a
hit. The \ult here is the pooled hit.

\subsection{Aggregation and operation}

An engine's headline number is $S(e)=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{b\in B}
M_b(e)/M_b(\text{ceiling})$, its mean bench share of the pooled ceiling: a share of an oracle list,
not of the web. Table~\ref{tab:results} uses runs since 17 October where every
configuration ran. A query issued in one run is a \emph{query-run}.

\textbf{Reading Table~\ref{tab:results}.} The bench cells are absolute metrics (best per column in bold), the
bar is the mean share of a pooled oracle, and everything is conditional
on scorer version
\texttt{paper-2026-10-30} (Appendix~\ref{app:examples}): (i)~news and rare are judged nDCG@5 on snippets; blinded raters
of three vendors agree with the judge at $\kappa$ .77--.79 and with each
other at .90--.94, and on 100 complete pools rank engines at Spearman
.94 with the judge (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}); (ii)~scholar and legal are identifier matches; (iii)~finance is answer presence under a 0.1\% money band
whose table reading audits at .70--.98 on a fresh window
(Appendix~\ref{app:finance}); (iv)~a service failure scores zero and an
account failure is excluded, which costs Claude search 35\% of its
query-runs; rescoring on the 6,801 queries no engine lost keeps the
broad order ($\tau_b$ .91), and only Bing ($+5.7$), ChatGPT search
($-3.1$) and Parallel ($-1.9$) move beyond their intervals
(Table~\ref{tab:ci}); (v)~intervals are run-level bootstraps; dropping the scored engine from the pool moves shares by at most .007.


\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\textbf{The spread is large and bench-dependent.} Overall shares run
from 14\% to 73\% of the ceiling. Paired run-resampled differences under the stated scorer
(Appendix~\ref{app:checks}) separate 187 of the 210 engine pairs
pointwise; the 23 unseparated pairs form four neighbourhoods along the
order. A Bonferroni adjustment leaves 179 separated (172 also under day
resampling and matched support) and three neighbourhoods (Exa--Brave
(LLM ctx), Linkup--You.com, Claude search--Bing) plus Bing--TinyFish;
only Keenable and Ceramic stay separated from all others.

\textbf{Body-detail queries separate engines most.} Most engines find a paper from its title (Figure~\ref{fig:results}a,
.71--.98); on body keywords Keenable keeps .72, Google .60, Exa .53,
ChatGPT search .08.

\textbf{The leaderboard is stable but not static.} Adjacent weekly
leaderboards over the eight weeks of Figure~\ref{fig:results}b agree at
Kendall's $\tau$ .80--.93 and the leader did not change; on the one
post-freeze day scored under the frozen rules Perplexity leads Keenable
72.4 to 71.3, within a day's noise (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}).

\textbf{Four vendor pairs share most of their URLs.} The median pair
shares .055 of its URLs (Jaccard; Figure~\ref{fig:overlap},
Appendix~\ref{app:overlap}); four pairs share .49--.64.

\textbf{Site restriction lowers caselaw recall.} The same 80 captions
restricted to courtlistener.com lose .37 recall@5 (paired; 16 of 18
engines; Appendix~\ref{app:opsres}).

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{Conflict of interest.} The authors build Keenable, which
leads the current leaderboard; on the one day scored after the rules
were frozen it is second to Perplexity (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}). Everything that produces the numbers is public: the
generator code, the judge prompts, the engine clients, every per-query result
with the judge's reasoning, and the workflow that runs them; every table
recomputes exactly from the archived ratings, while the judgments
themselves can only be approximated (Appendix~\ref{app:examples}).
Keenable receives the same snippet cap, the same single-request pacing
and the same judge as every other engine. The authors changed no
engine's benchmark client during the window, Keenable's included; the
paper's scoring rules were frozen on 30 October 2026, after the audits
of Appendices~\ref{app:finance} and~\ref{app:checks}; every result in this paper is a rescoring of runs collected before that
date. The public dashboard at the URL above still scores with the
production rules of Table~\ref{tab:rules}; the paper's rules will replace
them as a labelled scoring version, and until then the two sets of
numbers are not directly comparable. Whether
any vendor's production index changed during the window, Keenable's
among them, the benchmark cannot separate from ordinary development, and
that holds for every engine alike. Readers should nonetheless treat the ranking as a claim to
audit, and the artifacts exist so that they can.

\paragraph{Snippet-only evidence.} The judge and the matchers see what the API
returned, not the page. That view is what an agent sees before it fetches, and it keeps engines comparable, but it understates engines whose snippets are
terse, and recall on the known-item benches is a lower bound on finding the
identifier (Appendix~\ref{app:checks} checks that hits are the target page). Snippet labels
are known to diverge from full-text labels \citep{kazai2022less}, though
judging what the consumer sees has its own case \citep{turpin2009summaries}
and LLM judgments from short text track full-text judgments well
\citep{mohtadi2026summarization}. The pooled ceiling inherits the same bound.
Snippet length does matter. On the runs of Appendix~\ref{app:checks},
median snippets range from 116 to 2,000 characters; the six engines below
200 characters all sit in the lower half of the news ranking, yet the two
longest sit last, and the rank correlation between median snippet length and
news share is .33. Rejudging eight queries
with every snippet cut to 200 characters moves engine shares by .08 on
average and the order to $\tau_b=.73$ (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}); the
bench scores the snippet an agent actually receives, so we report this as a
sensitivity, not a correction.

\paragraph{The LLM judge.} News and rare scores depend on one judge model and
one prompt. The prompt follows public rater guidelines and emits explicit
gates and reasoning per judgment, and identical results are judged once so no
engine is favored by sampling noise. A second judge from another vendor ranks engines at Spearman .90, and
two blinded raters who also read the page agree with the judge at $\kappa$
.77 and rank the 21 engines at Spearman .89 on news but .71 on rare
(query-resampled 95\% intervals .74--.94 and .50--.94), while on 100
complete pools two further vendors rank them at .94 (.87--.97) with the
judge, .91 on rare (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}); the raters are LLMs of three vendors, not
humans, they read the first 3,000 characters of a page rather than the
document, with snippet and page in one prompt (a staged snippet-only pass
on 400 items is in Appendix~\ref{app:checks}), 6\% of pages were not
fetched, and they share the literal-quote rule with the judge, which lifts
agreement on quoted rare queries ($\kappa$ .82) above the unquoted ones
(.73), so this check bounds judge noise and, for the 1,130 fetched
pages, snippet-only evidence, not shared model blind spots. LLM judges can be fooled by query terms
in a snippet \citep{alaofi2024fooled}, favor systems that use a similar LLM
\citep{clarke2024llmbased,panickssery2024self}, and cap measured quality at
their own competence \citep{soboroff2025dont}. The production judge shares a
vendor with ChatGPT search and the second judge with Claude search; both
engines sit in the lower half of the news column, and the second judge rated
Claude search lower than the production judge did. Known-item benches do not use
the judge and show the same overall leader, which is some reassurance, not
proof.

\paragraph{Finance matcher.} The matcher's earlier 2\% money band and money backstop admitted
neighbouring quarters and related line items: an LLM audit put their
precision at .71 and .36, against .93 or higher elsewhere
(Appendix~\ref{app:finance}). Table~\ref{tab:results} therefore
uses a 0.1\% band and no money backstop, rescored from the archived
results; the change lowers finance recall by 2 to 11 points per engine and
reorders the finance column ($\tau_b=.83$ against the old matcher) but
leaves the overall ranking unchanged (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}). An audit of 100 sampled misses of the tightened matcher found the gold
value in 29, mostly unit-less table figures or parenthesized losses;
reading those recovers 19 at .91 audited precision, and
Table~\ref{tab:results} includes that reading; on a fresh window
(Appendix~\ref{app:finance}) the reading's precision is .70 if a figure
with an unlabeled period counts as wrong and .98 if only contradictions
count; bounding the inflation per engine, or dropping the reading
altogether, moves Firecrawl two ranks and no other engine more than
two. Weighted by field prevalence and each
engine's own money recall, the two errors do not cancel: reported finance recall runs 2 to 3 points high for the engines
above .80, about 1 point high in the middle, and up to 1 point low for
Claude search and Ceramic, which compresses the spread from .48 to .44
and swaps one adjacent pair (Appendix~\ref{app:finance}); the audits pool
engines, so per-engine matcher error is unmeasured.

\paragraph{Coverage.} Queries are English and US-centric (US geographies for
trends, US courts and regulations, SEC filers); other languages are
unmeasured. The equal weight of the five benches in the headline share is
a convention, not an estimate of agent traffic; Table~\ref{tab:results} gives the per-bench columns for any other
weighting, and Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} collects every alternative
view of the order. The weighting matters for the lead: 3.7 of Keenable's
4.6-point lead over Exa comes from the scholar column (rare .6, legal .5,
news .1, finance $-.4$); dropping any one bench keeps Keenable first
($\tau_b$ .86--.93 against Table~\ref{tab:results}) but shrinks the lead to
1.1 points without scholar (\texttt{bench\_weights.py}), and within
scholar the lead sits on papers under 30 days old and outside health
sciences (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}). On rare, the
order also depends on the query mix: Exa and Perplexity lead the
unquoted queries and Keenable the quoted ones (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}). The news bench samples 20 queries per hour; single runs are noisy
and should be read through the windowed means. Engines are called with default
settings, a paid key and one configuration per vendor in
Table~\ref{tab:results}; a vendor's best tier may differ. ChatGPT and Claude
search are model-side tools that retrieve and then generate; we score the
sources they return, so they are comparable on quality but not on latency or
cost. Every query, gold value, result and judgment is archived with the judge
model and engine list of its run, so any window can be re-scored offline
from the stored ratings; re-running the judge is not exactly
reproducible, because the published dates the APIs returned were not
archived and runs are not stamped with their code revision, so the
archive reproduces every table but only approximates the judgments
themselves (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}); the next release archives both.

\paragraph{Gaming and repetition.} Query texts recur across runs. In
the Table~\ref{tab:results} window, counting every query-run whose exact
text also appears in another run of the window, 11\% of news query-runs
recur (mostly within one day), 3\% of legal, 5\% of scholar, 17\% of
rare (sampled from a fixed filtered artifact) and 35\% of finance
(registry facts recur by design; \texttt{repeat\_queries.py}); the last two are learnable in principle,
and the finance facts are public registry entries in any case. The feeds
are public, so a vendor could crawl them ahead of the hourly run. The LLM projection makes the exact query unpredictable, and a
vendor that indexes the sources faster simply is fresher, which is what the
bench measures; a vendor that detected benchmark traffic and answered it differently
would not be caught, and snippets stuffed with query terms would raise
judged scores \citep{alaofi2024fooled}; the page-text rater check
(Appendix~\ref{app:checks}) is the current guard against the latter. Per-query artifacts let readers check for
either.

\paragraph{Dispatch and support.} The comparison is live but not
synchronized: each engine works through the query list in turn, so Claude
search and ChatGPT search receive a news query 5 and 7 minutes after
the fastest engine on average (Appendix~\ref{app:examples}). On requests
dispatched within 5 minutes of the first (96\% of 100,839 engine--query
pairs) the news order is identical and no engine moves by more than .01,
so the lag is not measurable at this scale; a synchronized dispatcher is
future work. Vendor response formats also change without notice: from
about 02:00 UTC on 30 October 2026, the day after the window closed,
Google (through Serper) and Jina returned \texttt{google.com/goto}
redirect links instead of result URLs for about 40\% of results
(Appendix~\ref{app:examples}), which URL-based matchers score as misses
until the clients resolve the redirects. Account-side failures remove 35\% of Claude search's query-runs and
smaller shares for Exa, Kagi and Parallel; rescoring every engine on the
same surviving queries over the whole window broadly preserves the
order ($\tau_b$ .91) but moves Bing by $+5.7$, ChatGPT search by $-3.1$ and Parallel by
$-1.9$ points (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}), so the single ranks of those three engines depend on support. A reader
of the dashboard sees ranks first; the support, failure and scorer notes
of this paper belong beside them, and the dashboard will carry them.

\paragraph{Ceiling as normalizer.} The ceiling is the best any evaluated engine
found, not the best that exists. Adding a strong engine raises the ceiling and
lowers everyone's share; shares are comparable within a window, not across
engine-set changes. Unlike a TREC pool, every returned result is judged, so no engine loses
credit for unjudged documents \citep{buckley2007bias}; the pool only sets the
normalizer. Rescoring each engine against a ceiling pooled without it
\citep{webber2009score} moves no news share by more than .013 and leaves the
order unchanged (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}).

\paragraph{Dispatch order.} Within a run the engines are queried
concurrently, and each engine works through the query list one request at
a time, so fast engines finish the list before slow ones reach its middle.
Across the 37 news and rare runs with per-query artifacts in the
Table~\ref{tab:results} window, the first and the last engine to
receive the same query are a median of 16 minutes apart (90th percentile
54 minutes), a spread that the two slowest engines create while the other
19 stay within a few minutes of the first, and a whole run spans a
median of 14 minutes (90th percentile 63);
Ceramic is dispatched first most often, Claude search and ChatGPT search
last. For hourly news the slowest engines therefore see the web up to an
hour later than the fastest, an uncontrolled freshness advantage. To bound it we rescored the 249 news runs of the window that record
dispatch times (4,959 queries, 100,839 engine--query pairs;
\texttt{lag\_subset.py}) on the pairs dispatched within 5 minutes of the
first engine: 96\% of pairs survive (ChatGPT search 1,958 of 4,959,
Claude search 1,531 of 2,857), the order of the 21 engines is unchanged
($\tau_b$ 1.0), and no mean nDCG moves by more than .009 (Claude search
$-.009$, query-resampled 95\% interval $-.023$ to $+.003$; ChatGPT search
$+.004$, $-.008$ to $+.016$). Restricting to the 3,728 queries whose
dispatch spread is under 10 minutes, or to the 216 runs that finish
within 15 minutes, also leaves the order unchanged with no mean moving
by more than .005. Within an engine, the rank correlation between lag and
nDCG has median $.003$ across engines. Mean lag is under 30 seconds for 18
engines, 2.3 minutes for Jina, 4.8 for Claude search and 6.6 for ChatGPT
search. A synchronized dispatcher
remains future work; at the present lags the effect is below the
resolution of the window.

\section{Judge profile and gates}
\label{app:judge}

The news and rare judge runs in two steps, both with the same model. A
\emph{profile} call sees the query and today's date and returns: query type
(\emph{broad}, \emph{specific}, \emph{list}); domain (health, legal,
financial, academic, tech, news, commerce, jobs, other); whether the query
carries operators; one to three \textsc{main} aspects, phrased as what a
document must contain and never as the answer; auxiliary aspects; and a content
archetype (\emph{ephemeral}, \emph{persistent}, \emph{evergreen}) that sets
the staleness rule. The profile is stored with the query and pasted verbatim
into every per-result call, so all engines are judged against one reading.

The \emph{judgement} call sees the profile, today's date and one result (title,
URL, published date if the API returned one, snippet capped at 2,000
characters) and emits a YAML block with eight gates and a rating. Gates:
integrity (pass, limit, fail), relevance (entity, jurisdiction, topic),
coverage of the \textsc{main} aspects, completeness (list queries only),
current-event freshness (dated events only), staleness by archetype, a domain
cap (health, legal, financial, commerce and others carry hard caps), and
operator compliance (quoted spans must appear verbatim in the snippet or
title; \texttt{site:} must match the URL host). A failed relevance or operator
gate forces 0; a failed coverage or completeness gate caps the rating at 2;
staleness lowers the rating by one or two levels, or to 0 for a wrong year or
entity status; a dated-event query whose result covers the entity but not the
event is capped at 1. The rating maps to the Needs Met labels as 0,1 FailsM; 2
SM; 3 HM; 4 FullyM. The judge is told to score facts against the page's own
text and authority, never against its own knowledge of the answer. The
prompts are in the repository under \texttt{src/needle/shared/prompts}.

\section{Leak and quality filters}
\label{app:leak}

\textbf{Scholar.} A title query needs at least four words and a specific
title (a digit, acronym, hyphenated term or five content tokens). A body
keyword query needs at least three words and at least two content tokens
absent from the title and abstract; a body question needs eight words and the
same novelty test. A tip-of-tongue query needs twelve words, no quotes, no
decimal or five-digit number, at most two content tokens shared with the
title, and no coined term (acronym, camel case, digit-bearing word) that
appears in the title or abstract. A paper enters only if all four queries
pass. \textbf{Legal.} A CFR query needs three words, no citation form
(\emph{CFR}, \emph{U.S.C.}, \S, ``section $n$'', ``title $n$''), no part or
section number of the gold section, and exactly one span of two to eight
words that occurs verbatim in the section text; the quoted variant keeps the
span in quotes, the others strip them. \textbf{News.} The
projection prompt refuses evergreen items and returns a 2--6 word query on the
specific event; query ids hash the text and the hour.

\section{Worked example of the ceiling}
\label{app:example}

The gains are a design choice, top-heavy so that one FullyM result
outweighs one HM result by half and an SM result by an order of magnitude;
Appendix~\ref{app:checks} shows the engine order under linear and binary
alternatives. For engine $e$ and query $q$ with ratings $r_1..r_5$,
\begin{gather*}
\mathrm{DCG}_e(q)=\sum_{i\le5}\frac{g(r_i)}{\log_2(i+1)},\\
\mathrm{nDCG}_e(q)=\frac{\mathrm{DCG}_e(q)}{\mathrm{IDCG}(q)},
\end{gather*}
with gains $g(4)=1$, $g(3)=0.667$, $g(2)=0.117$, $g(1)=g(0)=0$, and
$\mathrm{IDCG}(q)$ the DCG of the pooled ideal list. A bench score $M_b(e)$ is
the mean over scored queries in the window (a service-side failure scores
0), the
ceiling's score is the same mean over ideal lists (1 for news), and the
headline share is
\begin{equation*}
S(e)=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{b\in B}\frac{M_b(e)}{M_b(\mathrm{ceiling})}
\end{equation*}
over the benches $B$ the engine ran. Two engines answer
one query. Engine A returns $u_1$ (rated 4), $u_2$ (2) and $u_1$ again under a
tracking parameter (rated 3, but URL normalization strips the parameter, so the
repeat scores 0). Engine B returns $u_3$ (3), $u_1$ (4) and $u_4$ (1). The
\ult keeps the best rating per normalized URL, $\{u_1\!:\!4, u_3\!:\!3,
u_2\!:\!2, u_4\!:\!1\}$, in oracle order, so
$\mathrm{IDCG}=1+0.667/1.585+0.117/2=1.479$. Engine A scores
$(1+0.117/1.585)/1.479=0.73$ and Engine B $(0.667+1/1.585)/1.479=0.88$. An engine that returns fewer than $k$ results is scored on the list it
returned, against an ideal list that still takes up to $k$ from the pool.
Had no engine returned a result rated 2 or higher, $\mathrm{IDCG}$ would be 0 and
the query would be dropped from every engine's mean. Ties in the pool are irrelevant because only ratings enter the ideal. The
paper applies the duplicate penalty to every query. The production code
skips it when the query carries \texttt{site:}, a rule meant for one-host
result lists that can in principle let a list exceed 1; we rescored the rare
bench without the exception for Table~\ref{tab:results}, which changed 3 of
1,748 \texttt{site:} engine--query pairs, and no news query in the window
carried \texttt{site:}.

\paragraph{False negatives.} We also sampled 100 money query-runs the strict
matcher scored as misses, across engines and runs, and asked an LLM
annotator (Claude Opus 5.5) whether any of the five results states the gold
value for the right company, period and metric. It found the value in 29 (95\% interval .21--.39; near misses, a different
period or line item, in 2 more); 20 of the 29 were at rank 1 or 2. Nearly all are formatting the matcher does not read: bare
table figures in millions or thousands without a unit word (``801'' for
\$801M, ``185,808'' for \$185.8M), losses in parentheses (``(458)'',
``\$(3.98)''), and prose rounding beyond 0.1\% (``\$30 million'' for

that reading. About a tenth of the remaining misses still hold the answer, mostly as
prose rounded beyond 0.1\%; against this, .07 of exact-band hits and .09 of
extension hits are false positives. The error rates are stratum-specific, so we apply them by stratum

finance queries, headcount 8\% and the other fields 69\%. For each engine

and other-field recalls of the production matcher, which the rescoring
did not change) and its extension share $x_m$ among money queries, and
form expected false positives $.23\,[.07(r_m-x_m)+.09x_m] + .08 \cdot
.07\,r_h + .69 \cdot .02\,r_o$ and expected false negatives
$.23 \cdot .10\,(1-r_m)$ (false negatives were audited for money only, so
the other strata contribute none). The net correction runs from $-.030$
for Keenable and Parallel ($r_m$ .94 and .90) through $-.013$ for Brave
and Context ($r_m$ about .5) to $+.006$ for Claude search and $+.011$

finance spread from .476 to .435 and swaps one adjacent pair. The per-engine miss samples (0 to 3 recovered misses each) cannot show
whether the error rates differ by engine. Of the audit samples, the first

hits and the 59 other-field hits were drawn after the rules were frozen

reading's .91 is a development-sample figure.

\section{Ceiling and judge checks}
\label{app:checks}

The appendices use every active configuration, so names absent from
Table~\ref{tab:results}, such as Parallel Turbo or Exa Instant, appear here.

\paragraph{Leave-one-engine-out ceiling.} On every sixth hourly news run from
29 September to 29 October 2026 (111 runs, 1,909 queries with a non-empty
ceiling) we recomputed each engine's \ndcg against a ceiling pooled from the
other engines only. Because every returned result is judged, the ceiling can
only fall when an engine leaves the pool, so its share can only rise. The
largest change is +.013 (DeepSeek, which joined on 17 October and so has
578 queries); every other engine moves by .004 or less, and the engine order is identical (Kendall $\tau_b=1$). An engine
beats its leave-one-out ceiling on at most 1\% of queries. Pool bias in the TREC sense \citep{buckley2007bias} does not arise,
since no result is unjudged; the pool only fixes the normalizer, and the
normalizer does not depend on any one contributor. On the
Table~\ref{tab:results} window the same check for every bench
(\texttt{loo\_all.py}) moves no scholar, finance or legal share by more
than .007, no news share by more than .012 and no rare share by more
than .034 (ChatGPT search, Exa), swaps one adjacent pair on news and two
on rare, and moves the overall share by at most .007 with its order
unchanged. When removing an engine empties a judged
query's pool (its results were the only rated ones) the query is dropped
for that engine: 13 of 5,083 news and 25 of 1,133 rare engine--query
cases, at most 7 for one engine (Keenable on rare), and none on the
known-item benches, where the pooled hit is defined for every query.

Anthropic credit refusals); the 429 responses of ChatGPT search, Linkup
and TinyFish arrived while our plans were within their limits and count
as service-side, scored zero. Requests are retried up to three times with exponential back-off before
a failure is recorded (Appendix~\ref{app:examples}). The account-side
failures cluster by date, not by position in the query list: Kagi's
1,562 fall on 27--29 October (1,180 on the 28th), Claude search's 4,607
on nine days peaking on 23--25 October, Exa's 256 on four days and
Parallel's 105 almost all on 22 October, and each engine's failures split
evenly between the first and second half of the list
(\texttt{failure\_profile.py}; the 6,530 request-level annotations with
error type are in \texttt{audit/failure\_profile.json}). The production
dispatcher issues one request per engine at a time and the five benches
run at different hours, so the window's 429 responses arrived at one
in-flight request per account, unlike the paired and fresh runs of
Appendices~\ref{app:finance} and \ref{app:opsres}, which sent two to
four concurrent requests to some engines and provoked limits the
production runs do not reach; the archived \texttt{queried\_at} stamps
give the per-request timing. Account-side failures are excluded from the scores of Perplexity,
Exa, Kagi, Parallel and Claude search, while the Fail column of
Table~\ref{tab:results} still reports them. In particular, the collapse of
Claude search in the week of 19 October on the dashboard was our credit
lapse, not a change in the service. Exclusion assumes the engine would
have scored on the failed requests as it did on the rest; two imputations
bound the alternative (\texttt{exclusion\_bounds.py}): scoring every
excluded request zero or scoring it at the ceiling puts Claude search's
overall share between 32.8 and 69.1 around the reported 50.9 (29--45\%
of its requests excluded per bench), Kagi between 43.9 and 57.4 (49.6),
Exa between 63.5 and 70.6 (68.4), Parallel between 63.7 and 67.0 (65.1)
and Perplexity between 65.2 and 68.1 (66.6). Without the assumption
Claude search's rank is undetermined and Kagi's range spans ranks 9 to
15; Exa, Parallel and Perplexity keep their groups under either extreme,
though Exa's upper bound narrows the Keenable--Exa margin from 4.6 to

\paragraph{Empty ceilings.} A judged query that no engine answers with a
rating of 2 or more has an empty ceiling and is dropped: 69 of the 5,101
news queries in the window (1.4\%) and 176 of the 1,300 rare queries
(13.5\%; 149 of the 551 quoted and 27 of the 749 unquoted). Scoring those queries as zero for every engine and for the ceiling
instead lowers every absolute mean nDCG in proportion (by 1.4\% on news
and 13.5\% on rare) but changes no share of ceiling by more than .003
and no order (\texttt{ceiling\_drop.py}): the share is invariant because
the zeros enter the engine mean and the ceiling mean alike, so the
published scores condition on at least one engine finding useful
evidence and the absolute nDCG columns of Table~\ref{tab:results} should
be read as conditional means.

\paragraph{Where the scholar lead comes from.} The scholar column
carries most of the headline lead (Limitations), so we split it by the
paper's age bucket, domain and query bucket over the 12
Table~\ref{tab:results} runs (4,820 queries; \texttt{scholar\_strata.py}).
Keenable's recall@10 exceeds Exa's by $+.38$ on papers 7 days old (.79
against .41), $+.10$ at 30 days and $+.03$ at one year; by $+.20$ to
$+.30$ in computer, life, physical and social sciences but $-.10$ in
health sciences (.35 against .45, where Parallel leads); and by $+.01$
on title queries (where Parallel leads), $+.17$ on body keywords and
tip-of-tongue descriptions and $+.33$ on body questions. The lead is
therefore a freshness and full-text lead on recent non-medical papers,
not a uniform one. The production generator does not log how many
candidate papers fail the requirement that all four queries pass, so we
ran an instrumented copy of it once, on 30 October 2026 at 19:00 UTC,
with the production code and query writer (GPT-6) over the 15
domain-by-age cells, sampling 30 candidates per cell where production
samples 21 (\texttt{scholar\_rejects.py},
\texttt{audit/scholar\_rejects.json}). It sampled 614 papers from the
feeds; 42 fell to the title filter (35 generic titles, 7 without a usable
title or identifier) and 572 reached the query writer, of which 323
(56\%) passed all four queries. The rejections are the model writing no
body question (157), an unfetchable body (41), a body question that
repeats title or abstract terms (37), a leaking tip-of-tongue description
(8), a missing body keyword (4) and one model error. Acceptance varies by domain more than by age (Table~\ref{tab:rejects}):
.73 in health sciences and .70 in computer science against .41 in life
sciences and .44 in social sciences, and .58 at 7 days, .62 at 30 days
and .51 at one year. The quotas fix the proportions: each cell takes its
first seven accepted papers from 21 candidates (30 in the instrumented
run), so in the 12 scholar runs of Table~\ref{tab:results} every cell
holds 6 to 8 papers (one run has 3 in social sciences at one year and
one has 4 in life sciences at 7 days), except health sciences at 7 days,
which Europe PMC fills in only 8 of the 12 runs

The filter therefore changes which papers a cell holds, not how many: in
life and social sciences fewer than half of the candidates pass, so
those cells hold the papers whose bodies yield a leak-free question
and description, while health and computer science cells hold a less
selected sample. Whether the rejected papers would rank engines
differently is untested: they have no queries.

\paragraph{Known-item hits are the target page.} A matcher hit shows that a
returned result carries the gold identifier, not that it is the target
document. On the three scholar runs of 25--28 October (13,311 hits) the
identifier appears in the hit's own URL for 95.1\% of hits, and 84\% of hits
are an arXiv page. On the ten legal runs, the section number appears in the
URL of 94.0\% of CFR hits and 95.4\% sit on a primary legal host (eCFR, LII,
GovInfo); for caselaw, 62.2\% of hits carry the docket, cluster id or
citation in the URL and 86.8\% sit on a primary host (CourtListener, Justia,
supremecourt.gov, uscourts.gov), while the rest are commentary pages that
cite the docket (SCOTUSblog, Wikipedia, legal news). Because a primary
host does not prove document identity, an LLM annotator (Claude Opus 5.5,
engine names hidden) classified 100 caselaw hits drawn four per
configuration from the ten runs: 80 are the opinion or a court docket page
of the gold case on any host, 14 are pages about the same case (Wikipedia,
news summaries, SCOTUSblog case pages, an oral-argument recording, a motion
in the same docket),
5 are opinions in the same docket with a different filing date, and 1 is
a page about another case. Per configuration, 12 of the 25 had the
target page in all four sampled hits, 8 in three, 4 in two (Brave LLM
context, Kagi, Linkup, Octen) and Ceramic in none; four hits per
configuration bound rather than estimate per-engine identity rates.
Known-item recall is therefore a tight measure of finding the document
for scholar and CFR, and for caselaw it credits a page about the right
case in roughly one hit in six. Scaling every engine's caselaw hits by the
pooled target fraction .80 (Wilson .71--.87) lowers legal recall by .01 to
.05 and keeps the legal order at $\tau_b$ .94 (\texttt{legal\_target\_only.py});
applying each configuration's own sampled fraction instead, which four
hits bound only to within .25, gives $\tau_b$ .76, so the audit rules out
a large pooled effect but not engine-specific ones. In the other
direction, we sampled 100 scholar body and question query-runs the matcher
scored as misses and had an LLM annotator check whether the target paper is
among the ten results under a URL or snippet that carries no identifier:
it is in 11 (95\% interval .06--.19; project pages, aggregators such as
ResearchGate, SciELO and PaperPeel, a podcast page and a news summary),
never as a citation inside another document. Scholar recall is thus a lower bound by about a tenth of
the misses, with no engine favored in the sample.

\paragraph{Blinded raters with page text.} The checks above reuse the
production prompt. To test the judge against an independent reading, we
sampled 100 news and 100 rare queries from the 17--29 October runs with at
least 19 engines, drew six distinct top-5 URLs per query (1,200 results,
judge ratings 0:441, 1:120, 2:263, 3:226, 4:150), fetched each page
(1,130 fetched; the rest are login walls, blocked hosts or dead pages) and
gave two Claude Opus 5.5 raters a written guideline (0--4 Needs Met scale
with the same operator rules, in \texttt{analysis/audit/raters/}) and, per
item, the date of the run, the query, the URL, the title, the snippet and
the first 3,000 characters of the page, with the engine hidden, all in
one prompt. Each rater saw the 1,200 items in its own random order in 24
sessions of 50 and recorded a snippet grade and a page grade per item;
the guideline asked for the snippet grade first, but the page text was
visible while it was written, so the in-prompt snippet grade is not a
staged measurement (the staged check is below). The 128 items with a

\paragraph{Second judge.} We rendered the production prompts for every
unique result of eight news queries, one from each of eight runs spread over
the same month (803 results; 23 of the 25 configurations active then had
complete lists for at least six queries), and had Claude Opus 5.5 rate them
under the same instructions in six independent sessions, without access to
the production ratings. We repeated the exercise on the rare bench: eight
queries from the eight daily runs of 22--29 October, 566 unique results,
23 engines with complete lists for at least four queries. The artifacts do not store the
published date the API returned, so the second judge saw title, URL and
snippet only. To separate that input difference from the model difference
we re-ran the production judge (same model, prompt and temperature) on the
same rendered prompts without dates. On news it agrees with its own dated
ratings exactly on 61\% of results and within one level on 91\% (quadratic
$\kappa$ .80, query-resampled 95\% interval .67--.88), and its mean rating
falls from 2.43 to 2.19: the published date is a large input for news.
Without dates it agrees with the second judge at $\kappa$ .76 (.68--.82),
against .79 with dates, so the model difference is about as large as the
date effect. On rare, where dates seldom matter, the rerun matches the
dated ratings at $\kappa$ .97 and the second judge at $\kappa$ .91
(.81--.95). Table~\ref{tab:agree} summarizes agreement with the production
judge (GPT-6). On news, ratings agree exactly on 55\% of results and within one
level on 92\%; the quadratic-weighted $\kappa$ is .79 and the unweighted
$\kappa$ .44. On rare, where half of all results are rated 0 by both judges,
exact agreement is 73\%, within-one-level agreement 99\% and the quadratic
$\kappa$ .90; engine shares correlate at Spearman .87 ($\tau_b$ .73) with a
mean absolute difference of .061, and the second judge rates Exa, Linkup
and Parallel Turbo .12--.14 higher. The second judge is stricter: its
mean rating is 2.22 against 2.43, 68\% of disagreements go that way, and
82\% of all disagreements are by one level. Recomputing \ndcg for the eight queries under each judge, with the ceiling
pooled from that judge's ratings, gives engine shares that correlate at
Spearman .90 (Kendall $\tau_b$ .75; resampling queries gives a 95\%
interval of .76--.97 on news and .40--.95 on rare) with a mean absolute
difference of .064; the two largest drops under the stricter judge are Parallel Turbo and
Claude search ($-$.20 each), engines whose snippets often paraphrase the
event without stating it. Eight queries are few, so these engine-level
figures bound judge disagreement rather than estimate it precisely; as with
human assessors \citep{voorhees2000variations}, disagreement on items leaves
the system ranking largely intact. Both judges read the same precomputed query profile, so a profile that
misreads a query would not show up as disagreement. An annotator (Claude
Opus 5.5) therefore wrote its own profile for the 16 sampled queries before
seeing the production ones: 13 production profiles match in intent, two
are narrower (they demand a detail the query only implies) and one is
broader (it accepts any 2022 top-ten list of Arizona schools where the
query names one school); none misreads the query or states the answer, and
the query type differs for two. Profiles are stored with every query for
inspection. The second judge's notes locate most
disagreements in four gaps of the rubric: a title that answers the query over
an empty or navigation-only snippet (integrity fail at 0 against a title-only
cap at 2), reports written before the outcome a query asks about, how fast a
deal from earlier in the year goes stale, and whether a rumor confirms an
event. These are prompt fixes, not judge failures, and we will fold them into
the rubric.

\paragraph{Equal snippet budget.} To bound the effect of snippet length, the
second judge rated the same 803 results again with every snippet cut to 200
characters. Against its own full-snippet ratings, exact agreement is .60,
agreement within one level .94 and the quadratic $\kappa$ .80, with a mean
rating of 2.14 against 2.22. At the engine level the cap compresses the
field: engines with long snippets lose share (Exa $-$.22, Keenable $-$.14,
Perplexity $-$.13) and engines with short snippets gain (Bing $+$.14,
Context $+$.10, Google $+$.09); Kendall $\tau_b$ between the two engine
orders is .73 and Spearman $\rho$ .88, with a mean absolute share
difference of .084. Resampling the eight queries (\texttt{trunc\_ci.py})
puts $\tau_b$ at .34--.83 and the mean absolute difference at .06--.13;
the losses of Exa ($-.22$, interval $-.41$ to $-.08$), Keenable ($-.14$,
$-.23$ to $-.05$) and Brave (LLM ctx) ($-.12$, $-.22$ to $-.04$) and the
gains of Bing ($+.14$, $+.10$ to $+.19$) and Google ($+.09$, $+.04$ to
$+.15$) exclude zero, while the leader's margin over the second engine
does not under either cap ($+.01$, $-.12$ to $+.16$ with full snippets;
$+.11$, $-.08$ to $+.32$ at 200 characters), so eight queries establish
the direction of the length effect but not its size per engine. The
leader and the bottom three do not change. Snippet length is
therefore part of what the news bench measures, by design: an agent reads
the snippet the API returns, and an engine that returns more useful text
earns more. Readers who want a length-neutral view can rescore the public
artifacts under any cap.

\paragraph{Paired variants (30 October 2026).} To pair the variants on
the same item we took 80 filing-document, 80 caselaw and 80 CFR targets
from the window's finance and legal query sets, stripped every
operator and quotation mark to make a plain query, and wrote the quoted,
\texttt{site:} and date variants of each (\texttt{paired\_build.py}; the
date variant brackets the filing or decision date by 45 days), then ran
the variants of each target adjacently through 18 configurations in one
run per bench on 30 October 2026 (\texttt{paired\_analysis.py}, \texttt{audit/paired/}; no Kagi, You.com
or DeepSeek). The run postdates the window; Google and Jina returned redirect URLs
(Appendix~\ref{app:examples}) on 43\% and 36\% of their results, so their
rows in Table~\ref{tab:paired} measure that change too, and Keenable ran
without an API key, so the public rate limit refused 91 of its 320
finance requests (229 answered). On the 80 filing targets the quoted span raises recall@5 by .10 on
average (target-resampled 95\% intervals exclude zero for 9 of 18
engines; none loses), \texttt{site:sec.gov} by .35 (16 of 18 gain), and
the 90-day date window changes it by $-.02$, Linkup ($-.16$) and Brave
(LLM ctx) ($-.15$) losing and Exa ($+.14$) gaining beyond their
intervals, so the unpaired 7-point date cost does not survive pairing. On the 80 caselaw
captions, \texttt{site:courtlistener.com} lowers recall@5 by .37 on
average and for 16 of 18 engines beyond their intervals (ChatGPT search
$-.88$, TinyFish $-.68$, Google $-.66$, Firecrawl $-.65$, Bing $-.62$,
Context $-.61$, Exa $-.55$; Keenable $-.03$, Brave LLM ctx $-.03$, Brave
$-.06$ and Perplexity $-.07$ lose least), so the unpaired third of
Table~\ref{tab:opsres} is a paired loss on the same items, not a
difference between caption populations; the quoted caption changes
recall by $-.04$ on average (Brave $-.16$, ChatGPT search $-.24$ and
Keenable $-.09$ beyond their intervals) and the 90-day date window by
$-.11$ (Google $-.35$, Brave and Linkup $-.33$, Tavily $-.25$, Brave LLM

\paragraph{Operator compliance.} For every \texttt{site:} query we measured the share of returned URLs whose host is the requested one
(Table~\ref{tab:opseng}). It is 1.00 for 21 engines on both benches, .95 and .86 for Bing, .19
and .09 for Kagi and .06 and .03 for Ceramic. The last two are limits of
the vendor interface, not of the clients: Kagi's lens parameter
(\texttt{sites\_included}), its only host filter, does not restrict
results, and Ceramic's API takes a bare query string, so the client
strips the operators; passing them as raw text returns no results on all
20 test queries (\texttt{ceramic\_rawops.py}). Google complies fully and still answers .13 of the
\texttt{site:courtlistener.com} captions, so its gap is not operator
handling.

cannot be audited. Counting only \texttt{site:} hits on the requested
host, as Table~\ref{tab:results} does, lowers mean recall on
\texttt{site:} queries from .578 to .527 on captions, .660 to .642 on
CFR and .654 to .652 on filings; only Kagi (.91 to .00 on captions) and
Ceramic change, every other engine by under .01. Table~\ref{tab:opseng}
reports the any-host figures.
